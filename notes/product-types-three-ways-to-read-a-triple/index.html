Product Types: Three Ways to Read a Triple

A triple type τ₁ × τ₂ × τ₃ can be built three different ways, and each has a real tradeoff. A pair is a two-compartment box, so nesting one pair inside another means one compartment holds an unopened box: ((1,2),3) needs two projections to reach the 1, and that is not a notational quirk but a description of how it sits in memory, e.g. in OCaml a two-word block whose first word points at another two-word block.

Left- and Right-Associative

Left-associative: τ₁ × τ₂ × τ₃ ≜ (τ₁ × τ₂) × τ₃. No new machinery is needed since this reuses ordinary π₁/π₂, but the cost shows up in how many projections it takes to reach an element the further it sits from the end: π₁(π₁(x)) = τ₁ while π₂(x) = τ₃.

Right-associative: τ₁ × τ₂ × τ₃ ≜ τ₁ × (τ₂ × τ₃). Same idea, mirrored, so now the last element is the expensive one to reach.

Primitive n-ary Products

The third option drops the nesting: the triple is its own primitive type rather than built from pairs, so every element is one projection away and π₁, π₂, π₃ are all direct. This is what real languages use, but it costs the type theory something. πᵢ now needs a typing rule for every arity instead of one clean rule, and it cannot be a normal first-class polymorphic function, since there is no good answer to what the type of map π₁ would be, short of some form of ad-hoc polymorphism.

What Real Languages Do

Scheme and Lisp lists are right-nested cons cells: (1 2 3) is (1 . (2 . (3 . '()))), and cadr, caddr, cadddr spell out the literal car/cdr path to each position. Grabbing the last element of an n-element list is O(n) for exactly this reason.

OCaml and Haskell tuples are primitive, dodging the ad-hoc-polymorphism problem by not giving a general πᵢ at all: you pattern match every time instead, as in let first (a, _, _) = a.

Records and structs (p.x, p.y) are the real fix, indexing the product over labels instead of position. That gives one uniform projection rule regardless of shape, no arity-specific machinery required.

Notes

The tradeoff is really about what "the type of a projection" is allowed to depend on: position in a fixed nesting, an arity-indexed family of rules, or a label. Sum types sit on the other side of this same question, except elimination there has to branch instead of project.
