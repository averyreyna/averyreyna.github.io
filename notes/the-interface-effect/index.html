The Interface Effect

Reading Alexander Galloway's The Interface Effect, particularly this passage:

Objects are never humans to a computer, nor are they faces or bodies. In this sense the computer breaks with those arts (painting, photography, cinema) that fixate upon the embodied human form—the face, but not always, the hand, but not always—and its proximal relation to a world, if not as their immediate subject matter then at least as the absolute horizon of their various aesthetic investments. The computer has not this same obsession. It aims not for man as an object. The reason is simple: because the computer is this object in and of itself.

Working Through the Openness Argument

The chapter's harder claim is about open source: once a company like Google or Facebook open-sources something, that does not necessarily mean it is the real technical artifact they want you to see. Openness becomes a strategy, a mode of power rather than a challenge to it. TCP/IP is "open," but that openness is precisely what makes the network governable: permissive at the level of content, authoritarian at the level of structure. Notes

The takeaway I keep returning to: the internet as infrastructure requires openness as a baseline engineering condition: interoperability, shared protocols, open standards. So using "openness" as a political demand deploys a word already thoroughly colonized by technical requirements. The critical force of the concept is diluted before it is even deployed.
