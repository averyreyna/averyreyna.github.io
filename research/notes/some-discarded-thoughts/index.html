Some Discarded Thoughts

Re: They Aren't Vibing

I got reviews back from an ASE submission and in answering the questions from the review committee, it got me thinking about how to make sound choices for empirical studies investigated agentic coding with human participants.

  • What is novel about the insights that was not yet known by other related studies?
    • Our analysis of the specific prompting language/terms comes to mind first
      • Breaking down the actual words being used and being granular in that respect is one of the most novel aspects of the study to me
      • In other papers of agentic coding (and maybe even the ones coming out now) sort of abstract the prompt itself (with good reason, we don't necessarily want to study everyone's prompt history) and focus most of the discussion on CONTEXT.md, etc. files
  • Did the thematic analysis include an explicit search for negative cases or disconfirming evidence (e.g., instances of over-reliance, missed errors, or failed control)? If so, how did these instances influence or refine the final themes?
    • No, there wasn't an explicit search for these negative cases, but the frustrations of using agents came up in the user studies in several instances (even if it was just as simple as "sometimes Claude does [blank] and it's annoying")
  • How do the authors account for the potential recall and anchoring bias introduced by asking survey participants to report only on their "most recent" agentic task? To what extent might this single-episode focus distort the general findings on developer enjoyment and task suitability?
    • I think this is getting at how the recency of the task we asked for is not going to be representative of the median opinion or "way of working" of developers, even if they are experienced
      • Even if that were true, this bias isn't fatal since these most recent tasks were likely completed with strategies that were informed by multiple sessions with agents deemed "useful" by the user

Reading Corner

  • Monolift: Automating Distribution With the Tools You Have at Home (PLOS '25): I appreciated the paper's immediate acknowledgment of how decision-making at the architectural level can benefit from decoupling the application's development model and deployment model together; The design choice of not supporting arbitrary lifts and making the user provide an efficient solution makes a ton of sense. The whole point of this abstractio is to offload computation and you can't really do that if you don't know how much to specifically unload.
  • Exploring the Theory and Practice of Concurrency in the Entity-Component-System Pattern (OOPSLA '25): Unsurprisingly, Wikipedia had the best beginner explanation of ECS. Remembering that components are usually implemented as structs or classes, then its relationship with entities and how that fits into a system becomes much easier to conceptualize.
