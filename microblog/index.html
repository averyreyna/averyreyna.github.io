Ephemeral Domains/Websites

To say that I've been indecisive with my personal website would be an understatement. Ever since I decided to represent myself online, it has been hard to pin down exactly how I want everything to look. I'm also constantly inspired by other people's websites, always impressed with the ways text can be rearranged, spaced, and colored to decorate someone's corner of the internet.

I found this and eventually connected this:

You cannot possibly be ready to back up who you are on the internet with your own body because that internet self is not your body—its the internet's body.

While this quote seems pretty out there, the thesis being made still sticks with me, and I selfishly interpret it to validate my choices in not choosing, as crazy as that sounds. Very Exciting Times

I've been reading and really enjoying Exciting Times by Naoise Dolan. This novel follows Ava, an Irish-born TEFL teacher in Hong Kong, as she navigates maintaining two (sometimes loving) relationships. I appreciate Dolan's honesty first and foremost. I would go as far as to say Irish writers in general have access to this written candor compared to other European (notice how I didn't say British) writers. She has a way of writing an internal monologue that reads comfortably, with the clauses being balanced: short and varied, then running on.

The book's political commentary revolves around Julian, one of Ava's lovers, who's a banker by trade, making more than enough money to allow her to stay in his apartment rent-free while he's away in the UK for said banking job. It's made clear early on that Ava is a leftist, even questioning people's worldviews when they don't consider class. To her, it permeates through every facet of life.

This makes even more sense considering her background and associations with British wealth; she comes from a country that fell victim to a man-made famine warranted by supposed growth.

When talking to her mother, she says:

I enjoyed conversations where I wasn't attempting to persuade anyone, where I just said precisely what I thought. I got tired of making myself acceptable.

Don't we all.

Ironically, Julian's father Miles is a Marxist history professor (not a Marx historian, but a historian who likes Marx) at HKU. When Julian first brings Ava to meet him, the conversation turned cold and pointed, highlighting the more uncommon dynamic of a father attempting to educate his son to have more extreme views.

While he's been gone, though, Ava has been visiting Miles, subjecting herself to stories involving political figures she has little idea of, let alone relating them to each other for some broader historical mythos. In one of her visits she goes:

...I got more social capital from pretending [my directness] was on purpose, not least because it made people assume my compliments had to be sincere. [...] Smart people often did dumb things.

Needless to say, I will be finishing this book soon.

Unlikability as Opacity

Not only do I mainly read literary fiction, but the last five books or so followed complicated, sometimes labeled "unlikeable" by the general public, female protagonists—leaning more towards anti-hero.

This idea first came to me when reading My Year of Rest and Relaxation by Ottessa Moshfegh. I first heard about the book in 2020/21 from the newly formed "booktok" community on TikTok. Everyone, and I mean everyone, commented on how unlikeable the narrator was, sometimes to the point where they DNF'd the novel. Anyway, she wasn't that bad. Was she selfish, a little abrasive, and rude to most people around her? Yeah. However, the whole point of the prose and internal dialogue is to explain those actions. Can the author make you sympathize with or defend them?

Most people have thoughts they are ashamed of. No one has "perfect" thoughts. We are all fallible. I think the metaphor I'm going for is opacity. Like the more the author can provide excuses for actions while not completely absolving them of guilt, the more our belief is suspended to be excited for what's next.

Even more cynically, I don't think you'd like most people. Well, you may not agree with everyone's actions, but that doesn't mean they're any less likeable. We have to stop treating these fictional characters like they're real. Maybe the comparison is closer to the salient difference between mythology and the myths themselves. One describes the actual stories and the other value systems—the creatures committing atrocities are not supposed to pass the test of existence, they act as symbols. Maybe these characters are symbols themselves. Good writing and character studies teach us about the world. Can I really see where someone is coming from, even if they are unlikeable?

More on Motivation

I have felt a too-muchness when diving into fields like formal methods or program synthesis, subjects that are anachronistic in their applications and learnings. You can ask questions about user interfaces and stretch their concepts to the actual syntax itself (the brackets, the keywords, the symbols) to gauge where we can decrease the bottleneck in our gulf of execution as code writers. It's funny to think about how I got to this field by way of AI-assisted coding, fully obsessed with structured knowledge transfer between developer eyes and programming agents. I think I'm just fond of correctness and verification. While this quote from Flusser's Gestures (a collection of essays that ask heady questions like "Does writing have a future?") is a little too cynical for my taste, the gist of the excerpt still rings true. Every discipline feels like it's some applied version of the one below, abstracting more details in order to observe relationships between concepts more clearly.

The so-called humanities appear to be working on such a theory. But are they? They work under the influence of the natural sciences, and so they give us better and more complete causal explanations. Of course, these explanations are not and perhaps never will be as rigorous as those in physics or chemistry, but that is not what makes them unsatisfactory.

Notes from Making Abstraction Concrete

  • Dijkstra points out that the point of abstraction is to create a new semantic level in which one can be absolutely precise in depicting a phenomenon
  • AI research explores invoking the abstraction process in LLMs to define higher-level, reusable modules for managing complexity in domains such as system design
  • Abstraction is widely used in interactive systems, typically through GUIs guiding the user between levels of abstraction in order to get a feel for the entire system
  • Min et al. make a deliberate deemphasis of common associations with "higher-levelness" and/or simplicity; this is due in part to relativity: a user's conceptual interests shape what they deem as "easy" or familiar; this distinction is important because it is difficult to disentangle the process of abstraction from notions of generalization
  • More on that: this connects with the evolution of programming languages: is Python really "high-level" from a conceptual standpoint? I understand it's a ways away from being something more procedural, actually manipulating memory allocation. However, maybe it's something closer to opacity: the syntactic capabilities of a language make engineering tasks more/less abstract, with differing languages being good at different things (duh!)
  • Abstraction designs do not influence only a single stage of Norman's seven stages of action—they shape all stages—suggesting that introducing an orthogonal dimension of abstraction across the Gulfs may reveal richer patterns of how these designs influence interaction
  • A user's mental model and the system can span the gulf of abstraction not only by shifting to higher/lower levels, but by moving through a space
  • Random thought: can this be applied to actual programming language text? re: Slim Lim's thoughts on concrete syntax; only up until very recently, the only way to actually develop meaningful, substantial software was to manipulate code; sure, we have direct manipulation programs with robust type checkers to make writing code easier, but that still required at least some technical savviness—would a regular software dev be able to reason with those programs effectively? I guess I'm trying to get at the fact that we're realizing software engineering is not only about coding, and that rings true outside of an agile workplace; back to this opacity analogy: through natural language, now, you can be an expert in system design, and even if you're not technically literate or working on something outside of your scope, you're now able to "abstract" away code and focus on its interactions; and I mean "abstract" in the schema way, not the simplification way.
  • The emergence of LLMs has enabled ways to significantly reduce the costs involved in the sensemaking stages

FP (Can Be) Universal

The initial friction for writing somewhat complicated programs in functional programming languages (specifically OCaml) feels less abrasive than other static languages like C/C++, and definitely much more pleasant than dynamically typed languages like Python.

Because everything is an expression, you know that there are no side effects you need to worry about. Even writing programs that compute a factorial or Fibonacci sequence is much easier to wrap your head around.

The immutability afforded to us by mathematical foundations creates a shared language both technical and non-technical users can understand.

Moving Through Abstraction

I mean to write a proper blog post about this but I think we have the AI-assisted programming conversation all wrong. Writing code by hand and LLM-driven development don't necessarily have to stand as opposing forces. Good, proper software fundamentals are, and always will be, important as an engineer. I think it's misplaced to call vibe coding pure dopamine hits. Do some people just prompt for what they want and don't bother learning code or industry standard practices that make life easier? Yeah. However, code has always been an interface that requires deep technical knowledge (or whatever depth is required for the proper maintenance of software systems). Now that interface is malleable.

I'm hesitant to completely villainize those that appreciate handmade software and scoff at the idea of an agent indexing their codebase, even as part of its training data.

Coding is not solved. I don't care if someone from the Claude Code/Codex/etc. team tries to convince me otherwise. Did we forget that functional programming exists? Coding tasks go beyond business logic tasks. Feature-driven development is obviously the most common way software gets created.

(Somewhat) related to the previous point, technical communicators like Daisy Hollman are interesting to me. She's been contributing to C++ versions and was the programmer's programmer (her words). Hollman sees the utility of agentic workflows through a refreshingly grounded lens. Like if someone that was giving talks in template metaprogramming before the advent of ChatGPT is enthusiastic about LLMs, is there something we can learn? Is something missing in our worldview?

If we really wanna extrapolate, text will always be the interface that outlives technological innovation. The sci-fi fantasies of autonomous agents do not bode well for actual people who care about things like community, care, and friendship—i.e., any sensible person with a good head on their shoulders. We need a way to communicate with autonomy! The precision of language is more important than ever.

Data Hoarding

Currently reading about shadow libraries, and by extension, pirate archivists. First started reading Anna's Blog to get acquainted with the subject.

I somehow philosophically relate to the mission of these pirates; I often (non-conspiratorially) think about who controls digital media and what that means for cultural preservation.

It's funny to read about this a couple years out of college since I used my fair share of shadow libraries to get textbooks for those 3000-level classes that require insider editions for reading materials.

The inclusion of these repositories in the datasets of LLMs, especially at the early stages ~three years ago, is sorta paradoxical. The Common Crawl dataset has been tokenized by GenAI applications for what seems like forever. The (un-)paywalled content is a part of culture.

I mean... fundamentally this phenomenon is a natural response to the privatization of academia's pools of knowledge. Of course it's not all bad: the ACM Digital Library is now open access and having complete access to the conference proceedings of HCI venues, for instance, is on net a good thing.

Part of the reason why AI is so hated is because of the black box of it all. Do you think people really want a tech company promising the automation of thought? Do you think the average person can explain what "AI" really encapsulates? It's become a misnomer fueled by societal projections, sometimes rightfully so.

The cultural metabolism for new technology is fast-tracked if science communication is easy to understand and starts from a place of familiarity, likely rooted in history as context.

Everyone needs to read Calm Technology, especially technologists. Technology amplifies humanness by being invisible.

Edits: AI is a stand-in for quote-unquote "not thinking." Technological intervention of any kind in anything remotely creative is blasphemous. For the most part, I agree. You cannot call yourself an artist if your "art" is generated by a diffusion model. You cannot call yourself an author if your words are coming from a stochastic parrot. I'm hesitant to project any further than that. There is research being done on computational creativity. As such, the thinking models we have are underdeveloped and rightfully so since the proliferation of this technology in the cultural zeitgeist happened not too long ago.
