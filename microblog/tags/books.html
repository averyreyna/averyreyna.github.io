Very Exciting Times

I've been reading and really enjoying Exciting Times by Naoise Dolan. This novel follows Ava, an Irish-born TEFL teacher in Hong Kong, as she navigates maintaining two (sometimes loving) relationships. I appreciate Dolan's honesty first and foremost. I would go as far as to say Irish writers in general have access to this written candor compared to other European (notice how I didn't say British) writers. She has a way of writing an internal monologue that reads comfortably, with the clauses being balanced: short and varied, then running on.

The book's political commentary revolves around Julian, one of Ava's lovers, who's a banker by trade, making more than enough money to allow her to stay in his apartment rent-free while he's away in the UK for said banking job. It's made clear early on that Ava is a leftist, even questioning people's worldviews when they don't consider class. To her, it permeates through every facet of life.

This makes even more sense considering her background and associations with British wealth; she comes from a country that fell victim to a man-made famine warranted by supposed growth.

When talking to her mother, she says:

I enjoyed conversations where I wasn't attempting to persuade anyone, where I just said precisely what I thought. I got tired of making myself acceptable.

Don't we all.

Ironically, Julian's father Miles is a Marxist history professor (not a Marx historian, but a historian who likes Marx) at HKU. When Julian first brings Ava to meet him, the conversation turned cold and pointed, highlighting the more uncommon dynamic of a father attempting to educate his son to have more extreme views.

While he's been gone, though, Ava has been visiting Miles, subjecting herself to stories involving political figures she has little idea of, let alone relating them to each other for some broader historical mythos. In one of her visits she goes:

...I got more social capital from pretending [my directness] was on purpose, not least because it made people assume my compliments had to be sincere. [...] Smart people often did dumb things.

Needless to say, I will be finishing this book soon.

Unlikability as Opacity

Not only do I mainly read literary fiction, but the last five books or so followed complicated, sometimes labeled "unlikeable" by the general public, female protagonists—leaning more towards anti-hero.

This idea first came to me when reading My Year of Rest and Relaxation by Ottessa Moshfegh. I first heard about the book in 2020/21 from the newly formed "booktok" community on TikTok. Everyone, and I mean everyone, commented on how unlikeable the narrator was, sometimes to the point where they DNF'd the novel. Anyway, she wasn't that bad. Was she selfish, a little abrasive, and rude to most people around her? Yeah. However, the whole point of the prose and internal dialogue is to explain those actions. Can the author make you sympathize with or defend them?

Most people have thoughts they are ashamed of. No one has "perfect" thoughts. We are all fallible. I think the metaphor I'm going for is opacity. Like the more the author can provide excuses for actions while not completely absolving them of guilt, the more our belief is suspended to be excited for what's next.

Even more cynically, I don't think you'd like most people. Well, you may not agree with everyone's actions, but that doesn't mean they're any less likeable. We have to stop treating these fictional characters like they're real. Maybe the comparison is closer to the salient difference between mythology and the myths themselves. One describes the actual stories and the other value systems—the creatures committing atrocities are not supposed to pass the test of existence, they act as symbols. Maybe these characters are symbols themselves. Good writing and character studies teach us about the world. Can I really see where someone is coming from, even if they are unlikeable?
