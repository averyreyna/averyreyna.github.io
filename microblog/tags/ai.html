Thinking via Code

This is a very malformed idea, so treat this as a giant TODO in microblog form:

  • Everyone has the ability to think computationally. This is the crux of the whole "AI in Pure Math" debate. We have engineers at frontier labs simply typing in "take a crack at solving the Riemann Hypothesis" (not really, but you know what I mean) and spinning up an agent to complete the task
  • This is only a proxy to what I actually mean. If I were to strip away and not flinch, I think I believe truly abstracting code away is not entirely a bad thing. I'm not even talking about loop engineering or something involving a harness. If you have an idea and it requires executable code, the most important thing at that moment to realizing the next iteration of whatever idea is having something to work with.
  • The best use case for this type of analysis is prototyping. The more quote-unquote meta aspects of programming are now entering the mainstream
  • The brightest minds in computer science include those that reconciled the connections between programming and cognitive science/learning sciences/design. I immediately think of PARC: they originally thought of things ubiquitous with modern computing, e.g. graphical user interfaces, object-oriented programming, etc.
  • All that to say: there are some people who are cognitive scientists that are trying to reconcile with the other side of the quantitative spectrum, with LLMs now equipping them to create artifacts previously reserved for those that know how to navigate the user interface that is a programming language.

Moving Through Abstraction

I mean to write a proper blog post about this but I think we have the AI-assisted programming conversation all wrong. Writing code by hand and LLM-driven development don't necessarily have to stand as opposing forces. Good, proper software fundamentals are, and always will be, important as an engineer. I think it's misplaced to call vibe coding pure dopamine hits. Do some people just prompt for what they want and don't bother learning code or industry standard practices that make life easier? Yeah. However, code has always been an interface that requires deep technical knowledge (or whatever depth is required for the proper maintenance of software systems). Now that interface is malleable.

I'm hesitant to completely villainize those that appreciate handmade software and scoff at the idea of an agent indexing their codebase, even as part of its training data.

Coding is not solved. I don't care if someone from the Claude Code/Codex/etc. team tries to convince me otherwise. Did we forget that functional programming exists? Coding tasks go beyond business logic tasks. Feature-driven development is obviously the most common way software gets created.

(Somewhat) related to the previous point, technical communicators like Daisy Hollman are interesting to me. She's been contributing to C++ versions and was the programmer's programmer (her words). Hollman sees the utility of agentic workflows through a refreshingly grounded lens. Like if someone that was giving talks in template metaprogramming before the advent of ChatGPT is enthusiastic about LLMs, is there something we can learn? Is something missing in our worldview?

If we really wanna extrapolate, text will always be the interface that outlives technological innovation. The sci-fi fantasies of autonomous agents do not bode well for actual people who care about things like community, care, and friendship—i.e., any sensible person with a good head on their shoulders. We need a way to communicate with autonomy! The precision of language is more important than ever.

Data Hoarding

Currently reading about shadow libraries, and by extension, pirate archivists. First started reading Anna's Blog to get acquainted with the subject.

I somehow philosophically relate to the mission of these pirates; I often (non-conspiratorially) think about who controls digital media and what that means for cultural preservation.

It's funny to read about this a couple years out of college since I used my fair share of shadow libraries to get textbooks for those 3000-level classes that require insider editions for reading materials.

The inclusion of these repositories in the datasets of LLMs, especially at the early stages ~three years ago, is sorta paradoxical. The Common Crawl dataset has been tokenized by GenAI applications for what seems like forever. The (un-)paywalled content is a part of culture.

I mean... fundamentally this phenomenon is a natural response to the privatization of academia's pools of knowledge. Of course it's not all bad: the ACM Digital Library is now open access and having complete access to the conference proceedings of HCI venues, for instance, is on net a good thing.

Part of the reason why AI is so hated is because of the black box of it all. Do you think people really want a tech company promising the automation of thought? Do you think the average person can explain what "AI" really encapsulates? It's become a misnomer fueled by societal projections, sometimes rightfully so.

The cultural metabolism for new technology is fast-tracked if science communication is easy to understand and starts from a place of familiarity, likely rooted in history as context.

Everyone needs to read Calm Technology, especially technologists. Technology amplifies humanness by being invisible.

Edits: AI is a stand-in for quote-unquote "not thinking." Technological intervention of any kind in anything remotely creative is blasphemous. For the most part, I agree. You cannot call yourself an artist if your "art" is generated by a diffusion model. You cannot call yourself an author if your words are coming from a stochastic parrot. I'm hesitant to project any further than that. There is research being done on computational creativity. As such, the thinking models we have are underdeveloped and rightfully so since the proliferation of this technology in the cultural zeitgeist happened not too long ago.
